Core Idea

The lecture covers carbonyl-containing compounds: the carboxylic acid (R–COOH) and its four derivative families — esters, amides, acid halides and acid anhydrides. It follows nomenclature (aliphatic acids, di acids), physical properties and acidity (resonance and inductive effects), preparation and reduction of acids, formation of derivatives, then the preparation, reactions and examples of each derivative (aspirin, acetaminophen, nylon).

Learning Points

  • Five carbonyl families: carboxylic acid, ester, amide, acid halide (X = Cl, Br), acid anhydride.
  • Carboxylic acids typically have pKa 4–5: weaker than inorganic acids (negative pKa), stronger than alcohols (pKa ≈ 15–16).
  • Acidity is explained by resonance stabilization of the carboxylate anion (delocalized over both oxygens); the inductive effect of Cl increases acidity: trichloroacetic > dichloroacetic > chloroacetic.
  • Acids are prepared by ozonolysis, Grignard + CO₂, nitrile hydrolysis, oxidation of primary alcohols/aldehydes, and oxidation of Ar–CHR₂.
  • LAH reduces acids to primary alcohols; LiAlH₄ reduces esters to primary alcohols and amides to amines; Grignard converts esters to tert-alcohols.
  • Acid derivatives are interconvertible by nucleophilic substitution: acid chlorides and anhydrides react readily with water, alcohols and ammonia; amides are unreactive because of resonance.
  • Amides have exceptionally high boiling points (hydrogen bonding); N-alkylation lowers b.p. and m.p.

Key Definitions

  • Carboxylic acid: R–C(=O)–OH.
  • Ester: derived from an acid by replacing the OH group by an OR group (R–C(=O)–OR′).
  • Amide: R–C(=O)–NH₂ (or NHR, NR₂).
  • Acid halide: R–C(=O)–X, X = Cl, Br. Acid chloride = the chloride (e.g. acetyl chloride, benzoyl chloride).
  • Acid anhydride: formed from the combination of two acids and the loss of water (R–CO–O–CO–R).
  • Di acid (aliphatic dicarboxylic acid): given the suffix –dioic acid in the IUPAC system; many occur in nature and keep common names based on their source.
  • Lactone: cyclic ester. Lactam: cyclic amide.
  • Ozonolysis: O₃ cleavage of an alkene, then H₂O₂.
  • Ammonolysis of esters: ammonia converts esters to amides.
  • Neutralization reaction: acid + NaOH → carboxylate salt + H₂O. Substitution reaction: acid + alcohol → ester (OH replaced by OR).
  • Lachrymator: tear gas (benzoyl chloride).
  • Analgesics: painkillers. Antipyretics: fever reducers.

4. Physical properties of carboxylic acids

  • Hydrogen bonding · solubility in water (lower acids) · acidity (Tables 3 and 4).

5. Preparation routes of carboxylic acids

  • Alkene ozonolysis (O₃, then H₂O₂) · Alkyl halide → Grignard (Mg; 1. CO₂, 2. H₃O⁺) · Nitrile hydrolysis (H₃O⁺, heat) · Primary alcohol oxidation (strong oxidant KMnO₄; weak oxidant PCC gives aldehyde, then oxidation) · Ar–CHR₂ oxidation → Ar–COOH.
  • Benzoic acid from benzene (two methods): KMnO₄ oxidation of an aliphatic chain; Grignard reagent.

6. Derivatives of carboxylic acids (reagent → product)

  • SOCl₂ / base → acid halide.
  • RCO₂H / heat → acid anhydride.
  • R′OH, H⁺ / heat → ester.
  • R₂NH / heat → amide.
  • Base (e.g. NaOH) → acid salt + H₂O.

7. Cyclic derivatives

  • Lactones (cyclic esters): γ-lactone, β-lactone. Lactams (cyclic amides): γ-lactam, β-lactam.

8. Amide types

  • Primary (1°) R–CONH₂ · Secondary (2°) R–CONHR · Tertiary (3°) R–CONR₂.

9. Anhydride types

  • Symmetrical: benzoic anhydride, acetic anhydride. Mixed: acetic benzoic anhydride.

Acidity of carboxylic acids

  • Weaker than inorganic acids (e.g. HCl), much stronger than most other hydrocarbon-type compounds (alcohols, ketones, alkynes). pKa: carboxylic acids 4–5; inorganic acids negative; alcohols ≈ 15–16.
  • Both acids and alcohols are Brønsted–Lowry acids: they ionize to H⁺ and an anionic conjugate base with the negative charge on oxygen.
  • The carboxylate anion is more stable (weaker base) than the alkoxide anion: its electrons are delocalized over both oxygen atoms (resonance stabilized); the alkoxide is localized (not stabilized).
  • Greater anion stability shifts the dissociation equilibrium toward more anion → higher H⁺ concentration → higher acidity.

Reduction of carboxylic acids

  • LAH (LiAlH₄) reduces acids to alcohols: the initial product is an aldehyde, which is further reduced to an alkoxide anion; protonation then gives the primary alcohol.
  • Both carbonyl groups are reduced (di acid: oxalic acid → ethylene glycol).

Esters

  • Naming: the R of the OR group first, then the acid name with –ic changed to –ate (analogous to carboxylic acid salts).
  • Esters of simple acids have fruity odors (apple, strawberries, pineapples, apricots).
  • Ammonolysis: ester + NH₃ (dry ether) → amide + R′OH.
  • Reduction: LiAlH₄/ether → primary alcohol (RCH₂OH) + R′OH. Grignard (R″MgX) → tert-alcohol.

Acid halides

  • Prepared from acids with thionyl chloride or phosphorus halides (PCl₅, PCl₃, SOCl₂).
  • React rapidly with most nucleophiles (water, alcohols, ammonia); irritating odors.

Acid anhydrides

  • Two acids − H₂O (H⁺, heat). Mixed anhydride prepared from a carboxylate (acid + NaOH) attacking an acid chloride (acid + PCl₃).
  • Nucleophilic substitution of anhydrides: H₂O → 2 acids; ROH → ester + acid; NH₃ → amide + acid.

Di acids on heating

  • Maleic acid (cis) at 135 °C → maleic anhydride + H₂O; fumaric acid (trans): no reaction.
  • Phthalic acid + heat → phthalic anhydride; isophthalic and terephthalic acids: no reaction.

Amides

  • Nomenclature: similar to carboxylic acid; drop the –oic acid ending and replace with –amide.
  • Exceptionally high boiling points for their molecular weight; alkyl substitution on N lowers b.p. and m.p. by decreasing hydrogen-bonding possibilities.
  • Preparation: acid + NH₃/heat; acid chloride + NH₃/heat.
  • Not reactive because of resonance (carbonyl–amine resonance, structures A ↔ B).
  • Hydrolysis (H⁺ or OH⁻, H₂O) → acid + NH₃; slow, needs prolonged heating or acid/base catalysts. Reduction with LiAlH₄/ether → amines (RCH₂NH₂), an excellent way to make primary amines.
  • Nylon: a diamine + a diacid chloride give a polymer with amide bonds (polyamide).

Processes / Stages

Propene → propionic acid

Propene → (H₂O/H₂O₂) propanol → (PCC) propanal → (KMnO₄) propionic acid.

Alkene ozonolysis route

Alkene → O₃ → ketone (no oxidation) and/or aldehyde → oxidation → acid. Example: cis-2-butene → (1. O₃, 2. H₂O₂) acetaldehyde → acetic acid.

1-Chloropropane → butanoic acid (two routes)

  1. CN⁻ → CH₃CH₂CH₂–CN → hydrolysis (H₃O⁺, or NaOH/H⁺) → butanoic acid.
  2. Mg → CH₃CH₂CH₂MgCl → 1. CO₂, 2. H⁺ → butanoic acid.

Benzoic acid from benzene

  1. Method 1: benzene + CH₃Cl/AlCl₃ → toluene → KMnO₄ → benzoic acid.
  2. Method 2: benzene + Cl₂/FeCl₃ → chlorobenzene → Mg → phenylmagnesium chloride → 1. CO₂, 2. H⁺ → benzoic acid.

Reduction of acetic acid

1. LAH, 2. H⁺ → ethanol (primary alcohol).

Mixed anhydride (acetic benzoic anhydride)

  1. Acetic acid + NaOH → acetate anion.
  2. Benzoic acid + PCl₃ → benzoyl chloride.
  3. Acetate attacks benzoyl chloride (loss of Cl⁻) → acetic benzoic anhydride.

Nylon synthesis

n adipoyl chloride + n hexamethylene diamine → polyamide nylon (amide bonds).

Examples

  • Substituted acids: 3-bromobutanoic acid; 3,4-dichloropentanoic acid; 2-ethylbutanoic acid; 4-chlorobenzoic acid.
  • Esters: methyl acetate (methyl ethanoate, b.p. 57 °C); ethyl acetate (ethyl ethanoate, 77 °C); methyl butyrate (methyl butanoate, 102 °C); phenyl acetate (195.7 °C); methyl benzoate (196.8 °C); sodium benzoate (salt); propyl butanoate (butanoic acid + propanol).
  • Ester nomenclature: ethyl 2-chloro-3-oxobutanoate; isopropyl 4-hydroxy-3,5-dimethylhexanoate; methyl 2-butenoate; phenyl 3-oxobutanoate; ethyl 2-pentynoate; dimethyl malonate; tert-butyl cyclohexanecarboxylate.
  • Formation of esters: ethanoic acid + ethanol → ethyl ethanoate + H₂O; propanoic acid + methanol → methyl propanoate + H₂O.
  • “Smelly stuff”: methyl butanoate – apple; O-methyl butanethioate – strawberries; ethyl butanoate – pineapples; pentyl butanoate – apricots.
  • Ammonolysis: methyl benzoate + NH₃ → benzamide + CH₃OH; formic acid + NH₃ → formamide.
  • Acid chlorides: acetyl chloride (ethanoyl chloride) from acetic acid + PCl₅; benzoyl chloride (BZCl) from benzoic acid + PCl₃/PCl₅/SOCl₂. Acetyl chloride + H₂O → acetic acid + HCl; + EtOH → ethyl acetate + HCl; + NH₃ → acetamide + HCl; + acetic acid → acetic anhydride + HCl.
  • Anhydrides: acetic anhydride (2 acetic acid, H⁺/heat); maleic anhydride; phthalic anhydride.
  • Amides: propionamide (propanamide), benzamide, cyclopentanecarboxamide, N,N-dimethylcyclohexanecarboxamide, formamide, N,N-dimethylformamide, acetamide, N,N-dimethylacetamide.
  • Neutralization: CH₃COOH + NaOH → CH₃COONa + H₂O.
  • Reduction: acetic acid → ethanol; oxalic acid → ethylene glycol.
  • Nylon: adipoyl chloride + hexamethylene diamine → polyamide nylon.

Clinical / Practical Importance

  • Aspirin (2-acetoxybenzoic acid = acetyl salicylic acid): made from salicylic acid (2-hydroxybenzoic acid) + acetic anhydride.
  • Acetaminophen (Tylenol): an amide-containing drug. Aspirin and acetaminophen are given as examples of analgesics (painkillers) and antipyretics (fever reducers).
  • Adipic acid → nylon (commercial); nylon is a polyamide built from amide bonds.
  • Acyl halides have irritating odors; benzoyl chloride is a lachrymator (tear gas).
  • Amides are an excellent route to primary amines (LiAlH₄ reduction).
